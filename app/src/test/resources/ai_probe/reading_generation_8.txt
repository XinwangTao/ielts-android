In the past two decades, green roofs—vegetated layers installed on top of buildings—have shifted from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy. Cities such as Singapore, Toronto and Stuttgart now mandate or incentivise their construction, and global installed area has grown rapidly. The appeal is practical: a green roof absorbs rainwater, reducing pressure on drainage systems during storms, and its soil and plants insulate the building beneath, lowering both heating and cooling costs. Yet the technology is not a simple fix, and its benefits vary sharply with climate, building design and the depth of growing medium used.\n\nB. The environmental case rests largely on stormwater management. During heavy rain, a typical green roof can retain between 40 and 70 per cent of precipitation, releasing the remainder slowly over several hours. This delay matters in older cities where sewers overflow during sudden downpours, discharging untreated waste into rivers. However, retention performance declines as the soil becomes saturated; during prolonged wet periods, a green roof may retain almost nothing. Engineers therefore treat green roofs as one component of a wider drainage strategy rather than a standalone solution.\n\nC. The thermal benefits are equally nuanced. In hot climates, vegetation and soil shade the roof surface and cool the air through evapotranspiration, which can reduce indoor temperatures noticeably. In colder regions, the insulating effect is real but modest, and some studies suggest that snow cover on a conventional roof insulates better than a green one. Perhaps the most consistent gain is the mitigation of the urban heat island effect: dense clusters of green roofs can lower surrounding air temperatures by a degree or two, a difference that matters during heatwaves.\n\nD. Costs remain a barrier. Installing a green roof typically costs two to three times as much as a conventional roof, and structural reinforcement may be required on older buildings. Maintenance—weeding, irrigation and inspection of waterproofing—adds ongoing expense. Proponents counter that these costs are offset over time by extended roof lifespan, energy savings and reduced stormwater fees, but payback periods are often measured in decades, which discourages private developers focused on short-term returns.\n\nE. Policy design therefore becomes decisive. Cities that simply require green roofs without support often see cheap, shallow installations that deliver few benefits. More effective programmes combine mandates with subsidies, technical guidance and incentives for deeper, well-planted roofs.
