In the past two decades, green roofs—rooftops covered with vegetation, growing medium and waterproof membranes—have moved from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy. Cities such as Singapore, Toronto and Stuttgart now mandate or incentivise their installation, and the global green roof market is estimated to grow by roughly 15 per cent annually. The appeal is broad: vegetated roofs can reduce building energy consumption, manage stormwater, mitigate the urban heat island effect and provide habitats for insects and birds. Yet the evidence for these benefits is uneven, and researchers warn that poorly designed or badly maintained roofs may deliver far less than their promoters claim.\n\nB. The thermal benefits are the most frequently cited. Plants and soil insulate buildings, lowering cooling demands in summer and heat loss in winter. Studies in Mediterranean climates report summer cooling savings of 10 to 20 per cent, while results from colder regions are more modest. However, a roof's performance depends heavily on soil depth and moisture. A shallow, dry substrate can heat up quickly and, in extreme cases, transfer more heat into the building than a conventional dark roof. Irrigation, meanwhile, consumes water—a paradox in cities that promote green roofs as water-saving measures.\n\nC. Stormwater management is often presented as the strongest argument. Green roofs absorb rainfall and delay runoff, reducing pressure on drainage systems during heavy storms. Yet the retention capacity varies enormously: intensive roofs with deep soil may hold most of a moderate rainfall event, whereas extensive roofs with thin substrates may retain only 30 to 50 per cent. During prolonged storms, the substrate becomes saturated and behaves much like a conventional roof. Planners who treat green roofs as a substitute for upgrading drainage infrastructure may therefore be overestimating their reliability.\n\nD. The biodiversity case is similarly nuanced. Green roofs can support surprising numbers of invertebrates, particularly spiders and beetles, and some cities have designed 'stepping stone' networks to connect isolated populations. But species richness on roofs is generally lower than at ground level, and the communities tend to be dominated by a few hardy generalists. Roofs planted with a single sedum species offer little for wildlife; diverse, native planting with varied substrate depths performs far better. Maintenance regimes also matter, since intensive weeding or the use of pesticides can undo ecological gains.\n\nE. None of this argues against green roofs, but it does suggest that policy should be more discriminating. Blanket mandates that reward quantity over quality may produce roofs that look green but function poorly. Researchers increasingly recommend performance-based standards—requiring minimum substrate depths, native planting and long-term maintenance plans—rather than simple area targets.
