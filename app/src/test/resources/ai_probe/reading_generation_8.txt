In the past two decades, green roofs—vegetated layers installed on top of buildings—have shifted from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy in cities such as Singapore, Toronto and Stuttgart. Supporters claim they cool buildings, absorb rainwater and provide habitats for insects and birds. Yet the evidence behind these claims is uneven, and the gap between promotional language and measured performance has begun to attract scrutiny from researchers who argue that green roofs are neither a universal solution nor a wasted investment, but a technology whose value depends heavily on design, climate and maintenance.\n\nB. The thermal benefits are the most frequently cited. A well-watered roof can reduce the heat flowing into a building by up to 70 per cent compared with a conventional dark surface, lowering air-conditioning demand in warm climates. However, studies in temperate cities suggest savings are far smaller, sometimes under 5 per cent, because heat loss through the roof is already limited by insulation. The effect also varies with the depth of growing medium: shallow roofs of ten centimetres dry out quickly and lose much of their cooling capacity during heatwaves, whereas deeper substrates retain moisture and perform more consistently.\n\nC. Rainwater management presents a similar picture. Green roofs can delay and reduce runoff, easing pressure on drainage systems during storms. But their capacity is finite. Once the soil is saturated, additional rain passes through almost as freely as it would from a bare roof. Engineers therefore describe green roofs as a useful complement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional drainage infrastructure. In cities facing intense rainfall, the difference between a roof that holds twenty millimetres of water and one that holds forty may determine whether a street floods.\n\nD. Biodiversity gains are real but modest. Surveys in London and Basel found that green roofs support a range of invertebrates, including rare beetles and spiders, particularly when roofs are planted with diverse native species and connected to other green spaces. Isolated roofs, by contrast, tend to host common species already abundant at ground level. Design choices—such as adding logs, sand patches and varied substrate depths—matter more than the simple presence of vegetation.\n\nE. The economic case remains contested. Installation costs are typically two to three times those of a standard roof, and maintenance, often overlooked in policy documents, can be substantial. Yet green roofs can extend the life of waterproofing membranes by shielding them from ultraviolet radiation, and in dense districts they offer amenity space that would otherwise be unavailable.
