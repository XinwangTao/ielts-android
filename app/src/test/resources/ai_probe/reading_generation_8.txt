In the past two decades, green roofs—vegetated layers installed on top of buildings—have moved from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy in cities such as Singapore, Toronto and Stuttgart. Proponents argue that these living surfaces offer a remarkable range of benefits: they absorb rainwater, reduce the energy needed for cooling, filter airborne pollutants and provide habitats for insects and birds. Yet the evidence behind some of these claims is more uneven than promotional material suggests, and researchers are increasingly calling for a more nuanced assessment of when and where green roofs genuinely deliver.\n\nB. The thermal benefits are among the best documented. Studies in Mediterranean and North American cities have found that green roofs can lower indoor temperatures by several degrees during summer, cutting air-conditioning demand by up to 25 per cent in poorly insulated buildings. However, the effect depends heavily on soil depth and plant choice; shallow roofs planted with drought-resistant sedum, the most common and cheapest option, perform far less impressively than deeper 'intensive' roofs. Moreover, the cooling benefit is largely confined to the building directly beneath the roof, with only modest spillover to the surrounding street.\n\nC. Stormwater management is often presented as a decisive advantage. A green roof can retain between 40 and 80 per cent of rainfall, depending on the season and substrate, delaying runoff and easing pressure on drainage systems during heavy storms. But engineers caution that this capacity declines sharply once the soil is saturated, and in prolonged downpours the roof may retain very little. The technology is therefore best understood as a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional drainage infrastructure.\n\nD. The biodiversity case is perhaps the most contested. While green roofs can host a surprising variety of invertebrates, critics note that they rarely support the specialist species associated with ground-level habitats, and that the widespread use of sedum monocultures limits ecological value. Some cities have responded by requiring a mix of native wildflowers and varied substrate depths, and early results from such schemes appear promising. Yet the scale of the contribution remains uncertain, and green roofs cannot compensate for the loss of brownfield sites or mature trees.\n\nE. Perhaps the most persuasive argument for green roofs is not environmental but social and economic. They extend the lifespan of waterproofing membranes, reduce noise transmission and, in dense cities where ground-level space is scarce, create accessible outdoor areas for residents. The challenge for policymakers is to weigh these varied benefits against installation and maintenance costs, which remain substantial. As one urban ecologist put it, green roofs are neither a panacea nor a gimmick: their value depends entirely on thoughtful design and realistic
