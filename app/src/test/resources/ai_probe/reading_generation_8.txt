{"title":"The Quiet Revolution of Urban Night Cooling","passage":"A. For most of human history, the drop in temperature after sunset offered relief from daytime heat. In modern cities, however, that relief is disappearing. Asphalt, concrete and dark roofing absorb solar energy during the day and release it slowly at night, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island effect. Research suggests that dense city centres can remain several degrees warmer than surrounding countryside well after midnight. This persistent warmth is not merely uncomfortable; it disrupts sleep, raises energy demand for air conditioning and increases the risk of heat-related illness among vulnerable residents.\n\nB. One increasingly popular response is the deliberate use of lighter-coloured surfaces. Painting roofs white or installing reflective coatings can lower surface temperatures dramatically, and some cities have subsidised such measures on public buildings. Yet the strategy has limits. Reflected sunlight must go somewhere, and in tightly packed streets it may simply bounce onto neighbouring walls and pavements, warming pedestrians instead. Moreover, pale surfaces lose much of their benefit once dust and pollution accumulate, meaning that maintenance costs can erode the initial savings.\n\nC. A more subtle approach concerns the way cities manage vegetation and water. Trees provide shade and, through the process of transpiration, release moisture that cools the surrounding air. Studies in several European cities indicate that a well-connected network of parks and tree-lined streets can reduce night-time temperatures by a measurable margin, though the effect depends heavily on species, irrigation and prevailing wind. Water features such as fountains and small ponds offer similar benefits, but only where supplies are secure; in regions facing drought, prioritising ornamental water for cooling may be difficult to justify.\n\nD. Urban planners are also revisiting the geometry of streets. Narrow alleys that trap warm air can be redesigned to channel cooler breezes, while orienting new buildings to maximise night-time ventilation may prove more effective than any single material change. Computer models suggest that combining several modest interventions produces greater cooling than investing heavily in one. The challenge, researchers concede, is that such combinations demand coordination across agencies that rarely share data or budgets.\n\nE. Perhaps the most significant obstacle is social rather than technical. Cooler neighbourhoods tend to be wealthier ones, where trees are older and air conditioning more widespread. Low-income districts often have less greenery and housing that is harder to retrofit. Unless cooling policies explicitly target these areas, experts warn, the benefits of urban climate adaptation may deepen existing inequalities rather than reduce
