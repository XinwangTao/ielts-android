In the past two decades, green roofs—rooftops covered with vegetation, soil, and drainage layers—have moved from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy in cities such as Singapore, Toronto, and Copenhagen. Proponents argue that these living surfaces offer a remarkable range of benefits: they absorb rainwater, reduce the urban heat-island effect, insulate buildings against temperature extremes, and provide habitats for insects and birds. Yet the evidence for some of these claims is more robust than for others, and researchers caution that green roofs are not a universal solution to urban environmental problems.\n\nB. The thermal benefits are among the best documented. Studies in Mediterranean and northern European cities have found that green roofs can lower indoor temperatures in summer by several degrees, reducing the need for air conditioning. However, the insulation value depends heavily on soil depth and moisture content; shallow, dry roofs perform far less effectively than deep, well-watered ones. In winter, the picture is more complicated, as the additional mass can retain heat but may also increase structural loads that require costly reinforcement of older buildings.\n\nC. Stormwater management is often cited as a primary justification for green roof mandates. Vegetation and soil retain a proportion of rainfall, delaying and reducing runoff into drainage systems. Yet the proportion retained varies enormously with roof design, local climate, and the intensity of individual storms. During prolonged heavy rainfall, most green roofs become saturated and release water almost as freely as conventional roofs. Consequently, planners increasingly treat green roofs as a useful supplement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional drainage infrastructure.\n\nD. The biodiversity argument is perhaps the most contested. While green roofs can support a surprising variety of invertebrates and, occasionally, nesting birds, the species that colonise them tend to be common, mobile generalists rather than the rare or specialised species that conservationists most wish to protect. Some ecologists contend that green roofs may even function as ecological traps, attracting insects to sites where food and shelter are insufficient for long-term survival. Nonetheless, when designed with diverse plant species and varied microhabitats, green roofs can contribute meaningfully to urban ecological networks.\n\nE. Cost remains the principal barrier to widespread adoption. Installing a green roof typically costs two to three times as much as a conventional roof, and maintenance requirements, though modest, are ongoing. In cities where mandates have been introduced, developers have sometimes passed these costs on to buyers, raising concerns about housing affordability. The policy challenge, therefore, is not whether green roofs work, but how to ensure that their benefits—which accrue to the public at large—are funded
