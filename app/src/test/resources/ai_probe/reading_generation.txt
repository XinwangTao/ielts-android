The industrial revolution changed this, flooding streets with gas and then electric light, and for over a century brighter was assumed to be better. Yet in the past two decades, a growing body of research has begun to question that assumption. Light pollution—the excessive or misdirected artificial light that brightens the sky and disrupts natural cycles—is now recognised as an environmental pressure comparable in some respects to noise or chemical contamination. Unlike many pollutants, however, it is almost entirely reversible: a switch, a shield or a timer can remove it instantly.\n\nB. The ecological consequences are wide-ranging. Migratory birds, which navigate partly by stars, are drawn off course by illuminated towers and often collide with glass facades. Sea turtle hatchlings, programmed to crawl toward the brightest horizon, turn inland toward coastal development instead of the ocean. Insects, a foundation of many food webs, are killed in vast numbers around streetlights, and some studies suggest that artificial light can suppress their reproduction. Even plants are affected: trees near constant illumination may delay the seasonal changes that prepare them for winter.\n\nC. Human health has also entered the debate. Exposure to blue-rich light at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone that regulates sleep, and chronic disruption of the body's internal clock has been linked to metabolic and cardiovascular problems. This does not mean that all outdoor lighting is harmful; rather, the timing, intensity and spectrum of light matter. A well-designed streetlight that directs illumination downward and uses warmer tones can provide safety benefits while limiting what escapes into bedrooms and skies.\n\nD. Cities are beginning to respond. Some municipalities have replaced older fixtures with shielded LEDs and introduced curfews for commercial signage. Dark-sky reserves, where lighting is strictly controlled, now attract tourists and astronomers alike. Yet progress is uneven. Retrofitting is expensive, and poorly designed LED replacements can actually worsen the problem if they emit more blue light than the lamps they replace. Critics also argue that dimming streets may increase the fear of crime, although evidence linking lighting levels to actual crime rates remains mixed.\n\nE. What makes light pollution unusual is the speed with which it can be addressed. Carbon dioxide lingers in the atmosphere for centuries, but a badly aimed lamp stops polluting the moment it is adjusted. Researchers therefore describe night-sky restoration as a rare opportunity: a global environmental problem that citizens, councils and businesses can meaningfully reduce within a single generation,
