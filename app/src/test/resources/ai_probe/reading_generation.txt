{"title":"The Quiet Revolution of Urban Green Roofs","passage":"A. In the past two decades, green roofs—rooftops deliberately covered with vegetation—have shifted from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy. Cities as diverse as Singapore, Stuttgart and Toronto now mandate or incentivise their installation. The appeal is broad: green roofs can reduce building energy consumption, manage stormwater, mitigate the urban heat island effect and provide habitats for insects and birds. Yet the evidence base for these benefits is uneven, and researchers warn that treating green roofs as a universal solution risks overstating what they can realistically deliver.\n\nB. The thermal benefits are among the best documented. Vegetation and soil insulate a building, lowering heat loss in winter and reducing cooling demand in summer. A widely cited study of a retrofitted office block in Chicago recorded a 25 per cent drop in summer air-conditioning costs after a green roof was installed. However, the magnitude of savings depends heavily on building type, climate and roof design. In mild climates the effect may be modest, and poorly maintained roofs can lose much of their insulating value within a few years.\n\nC. Stormwater management is often presented as a decisive advantage. Green roofs absorb rainfall and delay runoff, easing pressure on drainage systems during heavy storms. But their capacity is finite. Once the soil is saturated, additional rain passes through almost as quickly as it would from a bare roof. For this reason, engineers increasingly describe green roofs as a useful complement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional drainage infrastructure.\n\nD. The biodiversity argument is more contested. Studies in Swiss and British cities have found that green roofs can support surprising numbers of invertebrate species, including some rare beetles and spiders. Yet the ecological value varies enormously with substrate depth, plant diversity and the roof's height and isolation. A shallow roof planted with a single species offers little compared with a deep, diverse 'brown roof' designed to mimic degraded urban habitat. Critics argue that green roofs are sometimes used to offset the loss of ground-level green space, a trade that ecologists regard as far from equivalent.\n\nE. Perhaps the most persistent obstacle is cost. Installation can be several times more expensive than a conventional roof, and maintenance requirements are frequently underestimated. Policy incentives have therefore become crucial. Where cities offer subsidies or allow density bonuses, uptake has risen sharply; where they rely on voluntary action, progress has been slow. The lesson, researchers suggest, is that green roofs work best not as a standalone fix but as one component of a coordinated strategy for sustainable
