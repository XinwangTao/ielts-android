The industrial revolution changed that, first with gas lamps and later with electric lighting, and by the late twentieth century many cities had become so brightly illuminated that genuine darkness was almost impossible to find. Today, roughly 80 per cent of the world's population lives under skies polluted by artificial light, and in Europe and North America that figure approaches 99 per cent. What was once celebrated as a symbol of progress and safety is now increasingly recognised as an environmental and public-health concern.\n\nB. The most visible victims are nocturnal animals. Migratory birds, which navigate partly by starlight, are drawn towards illuminated towers and frequently die in collisions; in some North American cities, switching off skyscraper lights during migration season has reduced fatalities by more than half. Sea turtles, which rely on the contrast between a dark horizon and a bright beach to find the ocean, are disoriented by coastal development and crawl inland to their deaths. Insects, meanwhile, are attracted to lamps in vast numbers, disrupting pollination and depriving bats and other predators of food. Ecologists argue that light pollution is one of the few environmental problems that can be reversed almost instantly, simply by turning switches off.\n\nC. Human health is also affected. Exposure to artificial light at night suppresses the production of melatonin, the hormone that regulates sleep, and chronic disruption of the body's internal clock has been linked to obesity, depression and certain cancers. Yet the evidence is not entirely straightforward: some researchers caution that many studies rely on self-reported exposure and that the relative contribution of light compared with other factors, such as shift work or screen use, remains uncertain. What is clear is that blue-rich light, emitted by the white LEDs now favoured for their energy efficiency, is especially disruptive to circadian rhythms.\n\nD. The response from cities has been uneven. Some municipalities have adopted lighting ordinances that require shielded fixtures, curfews for commercial signage and the use of warmer, amber-toned bulbs in sensitive areas. The small Italian town of Fulda, which redesigned its street lighting in the 1990s, is often cited as a pioneer, though its influence has been limited by the reluctance of larger cities to bear the cost of retrofitting. Critics point out that dimming streets can raise fears about crime, even though several studies have found little evidence that brighter lighting actually reduces it.\n\nE. Perhaps the most persuasive argument for change is economic. Street lighting accounts for a substantial share of municipal energy budgets, and simple measures such as motion sensors and timers can cut consumption dramatically.
