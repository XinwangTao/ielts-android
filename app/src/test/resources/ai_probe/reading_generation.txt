{"title":"The Quiet Revolution of Urban Nightscapes","passage":"A. For most of human history, the night was defined by darkness. The industrial revolution changed this, flooding cities with gas and then electric light, and for over a century brighter was simply assumed to be better. In the past two decades, however, a growing body of research has begun to challenge that assumption. Light pollution, once dismissed as a trivial aesthetic concern, is now recognised as a measurable environmental and public-health issue. Satellite data suggest that the artificially lit area of the planet is expanding by roughly two per cent each year, and that the light emitted per unit of area is intensifying in many regions even where the lit footprint itself is stable.\n\nB. The ecological consequences are increasingly well documented. Migratory birds, which navigate partly by starlight, are drawn off course by illuminated skyscrapers and collide with glass facades in large numbers. Sea turtles that nest on beaches disoriented by coastal lighting fail to reach the ocean. Insects, a foundation of terrestrial food webs, are attracted to artificial sources in vast quantities and either die of exhaustion or fall prey to predators. Some researchers argue that such disruptions may rival habitat loss as a driver of insect decline in urbanised regions, though the evidence remains contested.\n\nC. Human health has proved harder to pin down, but the picture is sharpening. Exposure to light at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone that regulates the sleep-wake cycle, and chronic suppression has been linked in epidemiological studies to disrupted sleep, metabolic disorders and, more tentatively, to certain cancers. The difficulty is disentangling light exposure from the many other features of modern life that affect health, such as shift work, diet and stress. What can be said with confidence is that the timing, intensity and colour of light matter: blue-rich light in the evening is far more disruptive than dim, warm-toned illumination.\n\nD. In response, a movement known as 'dark-sky' conservation has gained momentum. More than a hundred municipalities have adopted lighting ordinances that mandate shielded fixtures, curfews for non-essential lighting and limits on colour temperature. Critics sometimes object that darker streets mean less safety, yet the evidence for a straightforward link between brightness and reduced crime is surprisingly weak; several studies have found that increased lighting displaces crime rather than eliminating it. Meanwhile, the energy savings from efficient, well-directed lighting are considerable.\n\nE. Perhaps the most striking argument for change is cultural. The Milky Way is invisible to the majority of people living in urban areas, and a generation is growing up for whom the night sky is an abstraction. Astronomers warn that ground-based observation is increasingly compromised by skyglow. Yet the remedies are unusually cheap and reversible: unlike many environmental problems, light pollution can be switched off.
