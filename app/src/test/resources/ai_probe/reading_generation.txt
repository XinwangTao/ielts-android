Public health officials report that heat-related hospital admissions rise sharply during prolonged hot spells, with the elderly, outdoor workers and low-income households living in poorly insulated housing being most vulnerable. In response, planners have begun to experiment with a range of cooling strategies. Planting trees and creating green roofs are among the most popular, as vegetation provides shade and releases moisture through a process called evapotranspiration. Another approach involves replacing dark asphalt with lighter-coloured paving, which can lower surface temperatures by as much as 10 degrees on a sunny afternoon. Yet experts caution that no single measure is sufficient; effective mitigation typically combines several techniques tailored to local conditions.\n\nD. Despite these challenges, the growing awareness of urban heat has begun to influence policy at the highest levels. Several cities now require new developments to meet minimum green-space standards, and national governments are increasingly funding heat-action plans that coordinate public health, energy and urban planning departments.
