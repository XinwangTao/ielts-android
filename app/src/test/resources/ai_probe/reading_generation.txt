In the past two decades, green roofs—rooftops covered with vegetation, soil, and drainage layers—have shifted from architectural novelty to mainstream urban policy in cities such as Singapore, Toronto, and Copenhagen. Proponents argue that these installations offer a wide range of environmental benefits, from reducing building energy consumption to managing stormwater runoff. Yet as municipalities rush to mandate their adoption, researchers are beginning to question whether the promised advantages are as uniform as early studies suggested.\n\nB. The thermal benefits of green roofs are well documented. Vegetation and soil insulate buildings, lowering indoor temperatures in summer and reducing heat loss in winter. A widely cited study of a Chicago city hall retrofit reported energy savings of up to 30 percent during peak summer months. However, subsequent research has shown that savings vary dramatically with climate, building type, and roof design. In colder climates, the additional insulation may be offset by snow accumulation, while in hot, arid regions, the limited water available for irrigation can undermine plant survival and, consequently, cooling performance.\n\nC. Stormwater management is often presented as the most reliable benefit. Green roofs absorb rainfall and delay runoff, easing pressure on drainage systems during heavy storms. Evidence from Germany, where green roof technology has been refined for over three decades, indicates that well-maintained roofs can retain between 40 and 70 percent of annual rainfall. Nevertheless, retention rates fall sharply during prolonged or intense storms, precisely when drainage systems are most vulnerable. Critics therefore argue that green roofs should complement, rather than replace, conventional stormwater infrastructure.\n\nD. The biodiversity argument is perhaps the most contested. While green roofs can provide habitat for insects and birds, the ecological value depends heavily on substrate depth, plant diversity, and connectivity to surrounding green spaces. Shallow, sedum-based roofs—favoured for their low maintenance—support far fewer species than deeper, more varied installations. Some ecologists warn that promoting green roofs as biodiversity offsets may create a false sense of compensation for the loss of ground-level habitats.\n\nE. Despite these caveats, the momentum behind green roofs appears unstoppable. Cities are increasingly coupling mandates with financial incentives and design guidelines that encourage deeper substrates and native planting. The emerging consensus is not that green roofs are ineffective, but that their benefits are conditional—shaped by careful design, appropriate climate matching, and realistic expectations about what a
