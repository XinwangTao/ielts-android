In the past two decades, green roofs—rooftops deliberately covered with vegetation—have shifted from architectural curiosity to mainstream urban policy in cities such as Singapore, Toronto and Copenhagen. The basic design varies widely, from shallow 'extensive' systems supporting hardy mosses and sedums to deep 'intensive' gardens capable of sustaining shrubs and small trees. Proponents argue that these surfaces deliver a remarkable range of benefits simultaneously, addressing problems that are usually tackled by separate and costly infrastructure.\n\nB. The most frequently cited advantage is thermal regulation. Vegetation and soil absorb solar radiation that would otherwise heat the building's concrete structure, reducing the energy needed for air conditioning in summer. Studies in Mediterranean climates suggest cooling-related electricity use can fall by up to 25 per cent in buildings directly beneath an extensive roof. Yet the effect is highly context-dependent: in colder regions the insulating layer may modestly increase winter heating demand, and poorly maintained roofs can lose much of their efficiency within a few years.\n\nC. Green roofs also influence the wider urban environment. They retain rainwater, slowing the flow into drainage systems that are increasingly overwhelmed by intense storms, and they provide small pockets of habitat for insects and birds in otherwise sterile city centres. Perhaps most significantly, they mitigate the 'urban heat island' effect, whereby dense construction traps heat and raises city temperatures several degrees above surrounding countryside. However, critics note that the aggregate impact depends on scale; scattered installations on a handful of buildings produce measurable but limited results, and city-wide benefits require coverage that few municipalities have yet achieved.\n\nD. Cost remains the central obstacle. Installing a green roof typically costs two to three times as much as a conventional one, and structural reinforcement may be required on older buildings. Advocates counter that life-cycle analyses, which account for energy savings, extended roof lifespan and stormwater management, often show the investment paying back within fifteen to twenty years. Such calculations, though, rely on assumptions about future energy prices and maintenance standards that are difficult to verify.\n\nE. Policy has therefore become the decisive factor. Cities that mandate or incentivise green roofs—through density bonuses, direct subsidies or legal requirements on new commercial buildings—have seen adoption rise sharply, while voluntary schemes have generally produced patchy results.
