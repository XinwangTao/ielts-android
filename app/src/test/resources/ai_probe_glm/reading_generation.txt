In large metropolitan areas, the difference can reach several degrees Celsius, particularly at night. The primary cause is the replacement of natural surfaces such as vegetation and soil with concrete, asphalt and brick, materials that absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly after sunset. As a result, urban areas not only warm up faster but also cool down more slowly than rural landscapes.\n\nB. The consequences of this temperature gap extend beyond simple discomfort. Higher temperatures increase the demand for air conditioning, which in turn raises energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. During heat waves, the mortality rate among elderly residents and people with cardiovascular conditions rises sharply, and studies in European cities suggest that heat-related deaths are up to twice as common in the densest districts as in greener suburbs.\n\nC. Urban planners have responded with a range of mitigation strategies. One of the most widely adopted is the installation of green roofs, where layers of vegetation cover building tops. These roofs insulate buildings, reduce the energy needed for cooling by up to fifty per cent in some climates, and delay stormwater runoff after heavy rain. Another approach is the use of reflective, or cool, pavements that bounce sunlight back into the atmosphere instead of storing it as heat.\n\nD. However, such interventions face practical obstacles. Green roofs are expensive to install and require ongoing maintenance, which discourages private building owners from adopting them. Cool pavements, meanwhile, can produce glare that troubles drivers and pedestrians. Critics also argue that isolated projects deliver only modest benefits: simulations indicate that unless reflective and vegetated surfaces cover a substantial share of a city, the overall temperature reduction remains marginal.\n\nE. Some researchers therefore advocate a broader perspective, viewing heat management as a matter of urban design rather than of individual technologies. Wide tree-lined corridors can channel cooling breezes deep into city blocks, while protecting existing parks from development preserves the cooling effect of large green spaces. Evidence from Singapore and Medellín suggests that combining vegetation, water features and thoughtful building orientation can lower street-level temperatures by more than three degrees, a change
